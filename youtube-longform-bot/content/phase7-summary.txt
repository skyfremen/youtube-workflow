WORKING TITLE
Why Do You Forget What You Wanted After Walking Into a Room?

APPROXIMATE INTENDED DURATION
About 75 seconds for the current MVP long-form test.

LEARNING PROMISE
The viewer will understand how changes in context can act as event boundaries that reorganize memory, why this can make the previous goal temporarily harder to retrieve, and why the effect is not a magical property of doors.

STORY OVERVIEW
A character notices their phone is at 2% and launches a simple mission from the sofa: get the charger. They walk into the kitchen and instantly forget why they are there. Instead of treating this as a magical “doorway wipes your brain” fact, the story zooms into a playful memory-control-room metaphor. The viewer sees how the brain can divide continuous experience into events, how a context change can update what is currently most accessible, and why information from the previous event can become harder to retrieve. The explanation then adds the important nuance that event boundaries can both hurt and help memory depending on the task, and that a literal doorway is not required. The character walks back to the sofa, remembers the charger, and immediately creates a new problem by forgetting why they came back.

SCENE-BY-SCENE STORYLINE
1. Living room — The phone shows 2% battery. The character gets up with one clear mission: get the charger. A tiny personified mission note follows them toward the doorway.

2. Kitchen — The character crosses into a visually distinct room, stops, and looks completely lost. The narrator kills the “memory-erasing force field” joke and introduces the real idea: a major context change can mark a new event.

3. Memory control room — We move into an obviously metaphorical control room. The active event model changes from “sofa + dying phone + charger mission” to the new kitchen context. The charger mission is not destroyed; it simply slides away from the most immediately accessible slot.

4. Memory control room, strongly reframed — A split-behaviour demonstration shows the nuance. Boundaries can make details across events harder to connect, but they can also organize experience into useful chunks. The mission note is shuffled between the two lanes like an exhausted office worker.

5. Kitchen, over-the-shoulder — The doorway is shown as a strong context cue rather than a magical device. The narrator explains that event segmentation can happen without a physical doorway; the door is simply very good at announcing “new scene.”

6. Living room callback — The character walks back, sees the original context, and remembers the charger mission. Then they pause because they have forgotten why they returned to the sofa. The tiny mission note finally gives up and files for annual leave.

MAIN EDUCATIONAL POINTS
- The brain segments continuous experience into events, and context changes can help create event boundaries.
- Updating the current event can make information associated with the previous event temporarily less accessible.
- Event boundaries are not simply memory destroyers: research finds both costs and organizational benefits depending on the task and memory measure.
- A literal doorway is not required; context and event structure are the more important idea.

HUMOUR APPROACH / RECURRING GAG
Relatable “why am I here?” confusion is combined with a personified mission note. The note behaves like an employee being transferred between departments every time the context changes. The visual metaphor makes the memory explanation easier to follow without pretending the control room is literal brain anatomy.

FINAL PUNCHLINE / PAYOFF
The original context helps the character remember the charger — but now they have forgotten why they came back to the sofa. The mission note requests annual leave.

SCENE / LOCATION VARIETY AT A GLANCE

Repeated locations are either separated by a clearly different environment or returned to with different framing and story emphasis.
